1. Two Medicines, Two Questions

Modern medicine tends to ask what disease is this? Chinese medicine tends to ask what has gone out of balance in this person? The first looks for a cause and a lesion; the second tries to characterise the body's present state as a whole.

In the Chinese medical view the body is an interconnected system: the internal organs influence one another, the surface and the interior communicate, and the person is continuous with climate, season, diet, and emotional life. A symptom is therefore read not in isolation but in the context of the whole person.

Two principles follow from this and run through everything below: holistic view and pattern differentiation.

Holistic View

The parts of the body form one connected whole, and that whole is embedded in its environment. Local problems are usually expressions of a wider imbalance.

Pattern Differentiation

A pattern (zheng) is the combined state of a person at a given stage of illness. One disease may present several patterns; one pattern may appear across different diseases.

This explains a question that puzzles many newcomers: why do two people with the "same disease" receive different formulas? Because their patterns differ. Conversely, different diseases may share a pattern and therefore share a treatment approach — the classical idea of "treating different diseases by the same method."

2. Yin-Yang and the Five Phases

Yin and yang are not mystical entities. They are a way of sorting the two basic tendencies of any phenomenon: the active, warm, bright, and expanding is yang; the still, cool, dark, and contracting is yin. The pair is relative, mutually rooted, and each can turn into the other under the right conditions.

Health is described as a working balance between them. Imbalance shows up in recognisable directions:

  • Yang excess — fever, flushed face, thirst, agitation, constipation.
  • Yin excess — aversion to cold, cold limbs, pale complexion, loose stools.
  • Yin deficiency — heat in the palms and soles, night sweats, dry mouth, red tongue with little coating.
  • Yang deficiency — fatigue, sensitivity to cold, preference for warmth, copious clear urine.

The five phases (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) describe how organ systems generate and restrain one another: liver corresponds to wood, heart to fire, spleen to earth, lung to metal, kidney to water. Wood feeds fire, fire produces earth, and so on, while each also checks another. The value of the scheme lies less in the number five than in its being a model of regulation inside a system.

3. Organ Systems and Meridians

The Chinese "organs" (zangfu) are not identical to anatomical organs. They are better understood as functional systems. Grasping this point is the key to reading the theory.

SystemFunction in brief
HeartGoverns the blood vessels; houses the spirit, understood as mental and emotional activity
LiverMaintains free flow of qi and emotion; stores blood
SpleenTransforms and transports food and fluids; keeps blood within the vessels
LungGoverns qi and respiration; disseminates and descends; regulates water passages
KidneyStores essence; governs water; assists inhalation; linked to growth and development

Meridians form the network that carries qi and blood and connects the interior organs with the body surface. The twelve principal channels and the eight extraordinary vessels make up this map. Acupuncture and tui na work by stimulating specific points along it.

The organs of Chinese medicine are chiefly about manifestation — the outward signs of an inward function — rather than about a tissue under the scalpel.

4. The Four Examinations

Information is gathered by four methods. They are meant to cross-check one another; none is sufficient alone.

Looking

Spirit, bearing, body shape, and especially the tongue: its body reflects the state of qi and blood, its coating the nature of the pathogenic influence.

Listening & Smelling

Voice, breathing, cough; and odours of breath, body, and excretions.

Asking

History, onset, diet, sleep, emotions, stool and urine, menstrual pattern. Usually the largest single source of information.

Palpating

Pulse taking and abdominal palpation. Pulse qualities such as floating or deep, slow or rapid, deficient or excess reflect the movement of qi and blood.

Only after all four does a practitioner settle on a pattern — not a single symptom, but a judgement about location, nature, and the balance of resistance and deficiency. An error here throws off everything that follows.

5. The Eight Principles

The eight principles are the general framework of differentiation: four paired opposites that locate the character of an illness.

PairWhat it describesTypical signs
Exterior / InteriorDepth of the illnessExterior: chills and fever, floating pulse. Interior: involvement of the organs
Cold / HeatNature of the conditionCold: aversion to cold, pale tongue. Heat: thirst, dry stool, red tongue
Deficiency / ExcessBalance of resistance and pathogenDeficiency: insufficient vital qi. Excess: a pathogen that is stronger than the body's response
Yin / YangThe summary pairExterior, heat, and excess are yang; interior, cold, and deficiency are yin

In practice the principles combine: "exterior cold with interior heat," "deficiency and excess intermingled." Genuine cases are rarely a single principle, which is one reason pattern differentiation takes years to learn.

6. Treatment Strategy

Strategy sets the direction; method supplies the means. The classical formulations are compact: "treat cold with heat, heat with cold, deficiency by supplementing, excess by draining."

  • Support the upright, dispel the pathogen — strengthen what is deficient when deficiency dominates, clear the pathogen when excess dominates, do both when they are intermingled.
  • Root and branch — in acute situations address the branch first, in chronic ones the root.
  • Restore balance — reduce what is excessive, supplement what is insufficient, and let the pair return to relative equilibrium.
  • Adapt to the person, season, and place — the same pattern calls for different dosage and selection depending on constitution, climate, and locality.

The traditional list of eight methods — diaphoresis, emesis, purgation, harmonisation, warming, clearing, reduction, and supplementation — maps onto releasing the exterior, inducing vomiting, purging downward, harmonising, warming the interior, clearing heat, resolving stagnation, and supplementing deficiency.

7. Herbs and Formulas

Four natures, five flavours

Herbal properties are described by four natures and five flavours. The natures — cold, hot, warm, cool — give the direction in which a substance moves the body's temperature. The flavours carry tendencies: pungent disperses and moves, sweet supplements and moderates, sour gathers and astringes, bitter drains and dries, salty softens and moves downward.

Roles within a formula

A formula is a structure with a division of labour, not a pile of herbs:

  • Chief — addresses the main pattern; usually the strongest and largest in dose.
  • Deputy — supports the chief or addresses a secondary aspect.
  • Assistant — moderates the chief's tendency, reduces toxicity, or acts as a counterbalance.
  • Envoy — guides the action to a region or harmonises the whole.

Combination is about increasing effect and reducing harm. Two herbs that reinforce each other form a mutual accentuation; two that weaken or injure form an incompatibility. The classical lists of eighteen incompatibilities and nineteen antagonisms record accumulated experience with combinations to avoid.

A herb corrects a bias precisely because it has one of its own. Where there is no bias in the person, the medicine is the poison.

Later centuries continued the tradition: the four masters of the Jin-Yuan period and their competing schools, the Ming synthesis of yin-yang theory in Zhang Jiebin's Jingyue Quanshu, the Qing development of warm-disease theory, and Zhang Xichun's early twentieth-century attempt at a dialogue between Chinese and Western medicine in Yixue Zhongzhong Canxi Lu.

9. Common Misconceptions

  • "Herbs have no side effects." Untrue. Herbs have definite biases and contraindications; used wrongly they cause harm. The old saying that every medicine is one-third poison refers to exactly this.
  • "Chinese medicine is simply unscientific." The question needs unpacking. There is a large body of accumulated clinical observation, while the theoretical vocabulary of yin-yang and the five phases belongs to an older natural philosophy. The two vocabularies are different systems of description, and neither automatically refutes the other.
  • "It is slow and only good for chronic complaints." Inaccurate. Historical case records include a great deal of acute care — high fever, severe pain, vomiting and diarrhoea, bleeding.
  • "Following a published formula is enough." Risky. Formulas are modified according to the pattern; a formula that fits one pattern may harm someone with another. Self-prescribing from a book is not advisable.
  • "Pulse reading can diagnose anything." Overstated. Pulse is one of four examinations and must be combined with looking, listening, and asking. Conclusions drawn from the pulse alone are unreliable.
